You have infinite IQ. And you just woke up under a desk... in Mission Control. Houston, Texas. April, nineteen seventy. Rows of glowing screens. Men in white shirts and black ties. And a smell of coffee and cigarettes so strong you can see it. Hey! You're the new intern? Coffee's that way. This is Ricky. Fourteen. The janitor's son. He knows every bolt of every rocket NASA has ever built. In the next five days, you'll build an air filter out of cardboard, plastic bags and duct tape. You'll get sent to the basement by the smartest trainee in the building. And when the crew is freezing in the dark, he'll have to admit, in front of everyone, that he got the numbers wrong. So what if you had infinite IQ on Apollo thirteen?
First, the facts in your head. Apollo thirteen is the third mission to land on the Moon. Three astronauts. Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert, and Fred Haise. And two days after launch, an oxygen tank will explode, two hundred thousand miles from Earth. Nobody will land on the Moon. The only question will be whether anyone comes home.
You try to warn people. A kid nobody hired, under a desk, saying the oxygen tank will blow up. It goes exactly as well as you'd expect. Then you meet Chet. Nineteen. Engineering trainee, top of his class, and his father runs a whole department here. Kid, I've checked those tanks myself. Go get me a coffee. Black.
April thirteenth. Fifty five hours into the flight. The crew is asked to stir the oxygen tanks, a normal job. And then... a bang. Houston, we've had a problem. Alarms everywhere. One oxygen tank is gone. The other is leaking into space. The spaceship's main power is dying.
Why did it explode? Inside the tank, there's a little fan, with wires. Before launch, those wires got badly overheated in a test, and their insulation was damaged. Nobody noticed. When the crew switched on the fan... a spark. In a tank of pure oxygen.
So, turn around and go home? You can't. They're travelling at thousands of miles an hour, towards the Moon. There's no brake. So instead, they'll swing around the back of the Moon, and let its gravity throw them back towards Earth. Like a ball on a string. It's called a free return.
And the astronauts climb into their lifeboat. The little lunar lander, called Aquarius. It was built for two people, for about two days. It now has to keep three people alive... for four.
So Chet makes it official. The intern is in the way. You're sent to the basement, where the simulators are. Copies of the spaceship, for practising. This is the worst seat at Mission Control. It's perfect. Because down here, you can test everything before the astronauts have to try it for real.
Day two of the crisis. A new problem. Every time the astronauts breathe out, they make carbon dioxide. In a small cabin, it builds up, and it will poison them. The lander has filters that soak it up... but not enough for three people. The main ship has plenty of spare filters. But they're square. And the lander's filter holes are round. A square peg in a round hole, two hundred thousand miles from the nearest shop.
So you and Ricky tip out a box of everything the astronauts have on board. Cardboard. Plastic bags. Socks. Hoses from their spacesuits. And duct tape. Lots of duct tape. You build a box that lets the square filter breathe through a round hose. Engineers on the ground really built this, and read the instructions up to the crew, step by step. They called it the mailbox. The carbon dioxide level starts to fall.
Next, steering. To land in the right place, the crew needs to fire the engine for exactly the right time, pointing exactly the right way. But the computer is switched off to save power. So how do you aim a spaceship by hand? You use Earth. Keep the line between day and night on the Earth steady in the window, and you're pointing the right way. Ricky gets it first. So the planet... is the crosshairs? Jim Lovell really did this. Fourteen seconds of engine, steered by eye. Perfect.
The hardest problem comes last. The main ship has been switched off for days. It's freezing. Water is dripping down the walls. And to come home, they have to switch it all back on, using very little power. The power-up plan is Chet's. And down in the simulator, you run it. Three times. Every time... it uses more power than the batteries have. The lights go out halfway home.
Chet has already sent his plan to the flight director. If he says it's wrong now, in front of everyone, his name is on the mistake. If he says nothing... three men freeze in the dark. His pride... or the crew.
Chet looks at the numbers for a long time. Then he picks up the phone. Flight, this is Chet. My plan is wrong. The intern found it. We need two more hours. And for the next two hours, he and you rewrite it, switch by switch, until it fits. In the real mission, astronaut Ken Mattingly tested the power-up in a simulator just like this one, because he'd been taken off the crew at the last minute.
April seventeenth. The crew climbs back into the main ship and lets go of their lifeboat. Then they hit the atmosphere. Too steep, and they burn up. Too shallow, and they bounce off into space, like a stone skipping on a pond. The radio goes silent. Superheated air around the capsule blocks the signal. Normally, that lasts about four minutes. Four minutes pass. Then five. Nobody in Mission Control speaks. Ricky stops breathing. Six minutes. Then... Okay, Joe. Three parachutes, over the Pacific Ocean. The whole room explodes. Chet throws his tie in the air.
So, is any of this real? Almost all of it. An oxygen tank really exploded on Apollo thirteen, on April thirteenth, nineteen seventy, because of damaged wires inside it. The crew really used the lunar lander as a lifeboat. Engineers really built a filter adapter from cardboard, bags and tape. Lovell really steered a burn using the Earth in the window. The blackout really lasted around six minutes. And all three astronauts came home safely. Ricky and Chet are made up for the story. The real Mission Control team was full of people just like them.
Three things, in case it's ever you. When something goes wrong, first work out what you still have, not what you've lost. Test your plan before you trust it. And if you find a mistake, say so early. Even geniuses forget.
