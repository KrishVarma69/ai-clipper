You open a textbook. One glance. You close it... and you can still see every page. Your teacher reads the class list once. You know all thirty names. And their birthdays. Your best friend Leo loses his phone. You saw it three days ago... under the second sofa cushion. Leo. Behind you. How... how do you even know that? And the strangest part? You remember breakfast on your fourth birthday. Toast. Slightly burnt.
Your name is Max. A normal person forgets more than half of something new within a day. Scientists measured that back in 1885. You? You forget nothing. Not a word. Not a face. Not a number. Your memory got a hundred times sharper. But your forgetting... stopped working. You can save everything. You just can't delete anything. So what if your memory was a hundred times sharper?
Plus: school becomes a joke. Most people can hold about seven digits in their head at once. You hold seven hundred. Your teacher, Mr. Black, scribbles a long phone number on the board. You read it back. Backwards. Max. Did you just memorise my phone number? Sorry. Habit.
Languages? You learn Spanish in three weeks. Then Japanese. Then sign language, because why not? Dude. I've been learning French since 2019.
The best memory athletes on Earth memorise a shuffled deck of cards in under twenty seconds. You do it while tying your shoes. Leo shuffles. You glance once. Fifty two cards... in perfect order. Okay. That's officially creepy.
Faces? The average person knows about five thousand of them. You never lose a single one. A woman on the bus... you saw her once, at a train station, six years ago. She dropped a red glove. Sorry... you dropped this. Six years ago. She does not take it well.
Minus: you also remember every embarrassing moment. Every single one. Waving back at someone who was waving at the person behind you? Third grade. It still feels like this morning.
And arguments? You remember every word. Exactly. Your sister Mia snaps. I never said that! You did. Tuesday. Fourteen minutes past seven. You were holding a spoon. Being right... has never felt so lonely.
But where it gets really crazy... is the television show. A memory championship. Live. The prize? One hundred thousand dollars. And the producers want you.
And there's a cost. Every night, your brain tries to tidy up. While you sleep, it replays the day and decides what to keep. Yours keeps... everything. So you wake up tired. Heavy. Like a phone with zero storage left. Old pain never fades. A fight from five years ago stings exactly like yesterday. Normal brains forget for a reason. Forgetting softens things. You never get that softness. And lately... the headaches have started.
Then there's Nana. Nana is your opposite. Her memory is slipping away. Some days she calls you by your dad's name. Some days she asks the same question four times. You answer it four times. Every time... like it's brand new. But one thing never leaves her. A song. She hums it every time you walk past the old stone bridge. The bridge where she met Grandpa. You know every note by heart. Of course you do. She squeezes your hand. You remember everything, Max. Good. Then I'll remember for both of us, Nana.
Championship night. Bright lights. Final round. Three hundred faces. Three hundred names. You're winning. Then your phone buzzes. Mia. Nana's gone. She walked out an hour ago. It's four degrees outside, and she's only wearing a cardigan. When a body's core drops below thirty five degrees, that's hypothermia. Older bodies get there faster. The host turns to you. Max? Final question. One hundred thousand dollars... on the line. You replay every walk you ever took with Nana. Every street. Every bench. Every turn. Two hundred and forty walks. Your brain flips through them in seconds. Where does she go when she's lost? Not to new places. To old ones. To the bridge. It's two point four kilometres away. Walking, thirty minutes. Running... maybe twelve. The trophy... or Nana.
You pull off your microphone. The host stares. You're leaving? Now? You run. Cold air burns your lungs. You don't need a map. You've got two hundred and forty walks in your head. Then you hear it. Faint. Shaky. The song. Nana is on the bridge. Shivering. Humming. You wrap your jacket around her shoulders. She looks up... and for one moment, she knows you. Max? There you are. Back at the studio, the cameras were still rolling. Nobody cares about the trophy... anymore. And that night, for the first time in years... your head is quiet. Some memories don't need storing. They need sharing. I'll remember for both of us.
So... is any of this real? Partly. In 2006, scientists at the University of California, Irvine described a woman named Jill Price. She could recall what she did on almost any day of her adult life. Researchers now call it highly superior autobiographical memory, and very few people have it. But even they forget. A 2013 study found they still form false memories, just like the rest of us. And Jill Price has called her memory a burden, not a gift. So could a brain really remember everything... and still be happy?
